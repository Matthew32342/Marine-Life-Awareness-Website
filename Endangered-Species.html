Marine Life

Species That Need Our Help

North Atlantic Right Whale

The North Atlantic right whale is one of the most endangered large whale species in the world, with only around 380 whales remaining. They can grow up to about 16 metres long and weigh over 60 tonnes. These whales live in the North Atlantic Ocean and mainly feed on tiny organisms called copepods by filtering seawater through their baleen. They are endangered mainly because of entanglement in fishing gear, ship strikes, and changes in the ocean caused by climate change. Their slow reproduction also makes it difficult for the population to recover, as females usually have only one calf every few years. North Atlantic right whales are recognisable by the rough white patches on their heads, called callosities, and their distinctive V-shaped blow. Protecting them is important because their extremely small population means that every individual whale is important for the survival of the species.

Vaquita

The Vaquita is the world's most endangered marine mammal, with only a small number believed to remain in the wild. They are the smallest species of porpoise, growing to around 1.5 metres long and living in the northern part of Mexico's Gulf of California. Vaquitas mainly eat small fish, squid and crustaceans. Their biggest threat is becoming accidentally trapped in fishing nets, especially nets used to catch an illegal fish called the totoaba. Because vaquitas reproduce slowly and their population is so small, it is difficult for them to recover. Protecting them means reducing harmful fishing practices and keeping their habitat safe.

Giant Freshwater Stingray

The Giant Freshwater Stingray is one of the world's largest freshwater fish and can grow to several metres long and weigh hundreds of kilograms. They live in large rivers and freshwater systems in parts of Southeast Asia, including Thailand, Cambodia and Indonesia. They mainly feed on small fish, crustaceans and other animals found on riverbeds. They are threatened by overfishing, habitat destruction, pollution and the construction of dams, which can change the rivers they depend on. Protecting clean freshwater habitats and reducing harmful fishing practices is important for helping these impressive stingrays survive.

Great Hammerhead Shark

The Great Hammerhead Shark is the largest species of hammerhead shark and can grow to around 6 metres long. It is found in warm and tropical waters around the world and uses its wide, hammer-shaped head to help locate prey such as fish, squid and rays. The species is critically endangered, mainly because of overfishing and accidental capture in fishing equipment. Great hammerheads are also targeted for their valuable fins. They reproduce slowly compared with many fish, meaning their populations can take a long time to recover. Protecting them requires better fishing practices and reducing the demand for shark fins.

Hawaiian Monk Seal

The Hawaiian monk seal is a rare marine mammal found mainly around the Hawaiian Islands. They spend much of their time resting on beaches and feed on fish, squid, octopus and other marine animals. There are only around 1,600 Hawaiian monk seals remaining, making them one of the world's most endangered seal species. They face threats including loss of habitat, entanglement in fishing gear, disease, pollution and human disturbance. Rising sea levels can also reduce the beaches where they rest and raise their young. Protecting their coastal habitat and reducing human disturbance can help the population recover.

Atlantic Humpback

The Atlantic humpback whale is a large baleen whale that can grow to around 15 metres long and is famous for its powerful jumps and complex songs. They migrate between colder feeding areas and warmer breeding areas, feeding mainly on krill and small fish. Although humpback whales have recovered from historical commercial whaling in many areas, they still face threats such as entanglement in fishing gear, ship strikes, ocean pollution and climate change. Changes in ocean temperatures can affect where their food is found, making it harder for them to feed. Protecting their feeding and breeding areas and reducing human-related threats helps keep these whales safe.

Why Are Marine Life Endangered

Over-Fishing

Overfishing happens when people catch fish and other marine animals faster than their populations can naturally reproduce. This reduces the number of animals in the ocean and can cause some species to become endangered or even disappear from certain areas. Overfishing can also affect the rest of the food chain because many marine animals depend on fish for food. It can damage ecosystems and make it harder for fish populations to recover. Reducing overfishing through better fishing limits and sustainable fishing practices can help protect marine life and keep ocean ecosystems healthy.

Pollution

Marine pollution occurs when harmful substances such as plastic, chemicals, oil and rubbish enter the ocean. Pollution can injure or kill marine animals when they eat rubbish or become trapped in it. Chemicals and other pollutants can also contaminate the water and damage habitats such as coral reefs and seagrass beds. Plastic pollution is especially harmful because it can remain in the environment for many years. Reducing waste, improving waste management and preventing pollution from reaching the ocean can help protect marine life and keep the ocean healthy.

Climate Change

Climate change is changing ocean temperatures and conditions, affecting marine habitats and species.
